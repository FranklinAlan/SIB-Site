SIB Evidence Lab
Facilitator guide

Teaching goal: Explain what a study supports, trace where a claim came from, and write an update that stays within the evidence.

Open the gameOpen independent mini-casesStaged practice sheetsResearch applied
Version 3.2 implements all six pedagogical revisions: reasoning before supplied answers; accurate corrective feedback; a fourth independent task; a student-written evidence note; an experimental comparison in the debrief; and two reflection prompts with typed, paper, and oral routes. It retains the fictional FocusBoost case. This activity has not been validated as an effective intervention.

Prepare and run the activity

  1. Keep the seven supplied files together. Open index.html after extracting the ZIP, or replace the corresponding files in your existing GitHub Pages repository. No installation or external assets are required.
  2. Begin with approximately 6–8 individual walkthroughs as a practical discovery sample, not a validated sample-size rule. Include classroom phones, keyboard-only use, and relevant assistive technology.
  3. The game’s working target remains about 20–25 minutes, but the new sequence needs timing checks. For initial scheduling, allow up to 30 minutes for the game, up to 15 for the expanded assessment, and 5 for the debrief. These are provisional allowances, not measured durations. Record actual time and writing burden.
  4. Give the neutral briefing. Students make their first responses individually. Delay discussion and the debrief until independent responses are saved.
  5. If using paper practice sheets, distribute Stage 1 only. Collect it or confirm it is complete before releasing Stage 2. Release Stage 3 after the editorial decision. Do not distribute all stages together: Stage 2 contains supplied reasons.
  6. For assessment, provide assessment.html separately after the game and before discussion. It includes A–D, has no key or corrective feedback, and does not reopen the game automatically. Print a blank copy for paper assessment if required.
  7. Save required records under your normal classroom/research procedure. Then discuss the cases and the new experimental contrast. The game and assessment reset independently.

Neutral briefing

“You are reviewing a post for a campus psychology club. Some claims are supported by their evidence, some are contradicted, and some remain unresolved. Check what each claim says, inspect the available information, and decide what the club can responsibly communicate. Make your first responses on your own. There is no time bonus.”

Do not tell students to distrust everything or lower confidence. Give interface help without revealing an answer. Record reasoning help separately.

Student sequence

  1. Investigate and explain: inspect the original study, graph, and supplied citation trails. Save two judgments and an explanation, or explicitly skip the writing. No supplied reason choices are rendered at this stage.
  2. Compare explanations: the initial responses are locked and redisplayed. Students now choose a reason and count the studies. These later selections cannot overwrite the earlier explanation.
  3. Review feedback: correct and incorrect answers receive explanations. Source feedback describes the count, not an unobserved tracing action. An incorrect selected reason is subordinate to the corrective heading.
  4. Choose a headline and evidence: revise unsupported choices with feedback. First selections remain recorded. Eventual correctness is supported practice.
  5. Write the evidence note: save a brief draft, review the three-question checklist, and optionally revise. The first saved draft and final saved version remain separate. Students may explicitly skip the note. No keywords or automated model judge the writing.
  6. Make the editorial decision: publish the reviewed headline or hold the original causal wording. Both can be justified. The final artifact labels student text “Your draft evidence note” and separates it from the model note.
  7. Reflect: revisit the original explanation, choose retain/clarify/revise/unsure, and respond to two prompts. Type, use agreed paper, respond orally with the facilitator, or explicitly skip.

The original explanation follows access to the evidence and is not an independent pretest. Headline, editorial, and reflection work follows feedback. Completion is not a learning score.

Facilitate reflection and alternative response modes

Ask which evidence justifies the student’s current position. Retention, clarification, revision, and uncertainty are all legitimate positions; do not praise change merely because it is change.

  1. What is your reasoning now, and why? Explain what you would retain, clarify, or revise—or what remains uncertain. Refer to one specific piece of evidence. Brief bullets are welcome.
  2. What further evidence, if any, would help? Name the evidence and explain what it could resolve. If no additional evidence is needed for the present conclusion, explain why.

The digital paper/oral routes require students to report that they have already completed both prompts as agreed. Exports identify external_reported and the response mode. The page does not record the external response content or verify it. Keep the separate paper/facilitator record, including the assigned code if used, mode, whether both prompts were completed, a faithful response record, and any assistance. A paper/oral response is not a typed omission; an unverified self-report is not verified completion. Do not silently fill missing text with zero scores.

For an oral response, read the same prompts without supplying the reasoning. Record the student’s words or an agreed faithful summary under your normal procedure. No audio recording is built in. Paper and oral routes need a facilitator; students working alone may type or skip.

These are retrospective accounts, not direct evidence of a cognitive mechanism, confidence calibration, or a PCT effect. If comparing with a plain lesson, match content, explanation opportunities, feedback, writing, reflection routes, and time across conditions.

Connection to the original SIB workshop

The supplied Educational Pilot Rationale, Design and Methods documents three useful principles: learners express their own reasoning, examine feedback, and may retain or reconsider an interpretation. It also records that original answers were not redisplayed at the later reconstruction. This game therefore shows the original explanation again instead of relying entirely on recall.

The original workshop used comparisons of three video-judgment experiences and participant-generated contrasting distinctions. A single written explanation and reflection do not reproduce that structured elicitation. Describe this game as an educational extension of SIB’s attention to judgment, feedback, and reconsideration; do not present it as a replication or a test of a PCT mechanism.

The supplied numbered literature guide identifies Chi et al. (1994) as a precedent for prompting explanations and Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) for students actively interpreting feedback. These support design choices, not an established effect of this game. Explanations describe reported reasoning; use independent new-case performance to assess learning. Confidence and calibration require additional measures and a separate plan.

FocusBoost evidence key

All case details are fictional. App users averaged 78 and nonusers averaged 70 on the same exam. Students chose app use, app users reported more studying, and the analysis did not adjust for other differences. No sample size or uncertainty estimate is supplied.

PromptKey and reasoning
Users averaged eight points more.Supported. 78 − 70 = 8. The claim describes these observed means; it does not assert a statistically reliable population difference.
Using the app caused the difference.Unresolved. App use and other group differences were not isolated.
Reason“The score gap mixes app use with other differences between groups.”
Independent studies behind two postsOne. Study Sprint cites survey FB-01. Campus Catch-up cites Study Sprint.
Headline“FocusBoost users averaged eight points higher in this study.” The final bulletin adds the causal limitation.
Decisive editorial evidence“The group averages describe a difference; self-selection leaves its cause unresolved.” Both final actions are acceptable with this evidence.

Key distinctions: The graph is accurate within the fictional packet. The causal interpretation is not established. Reported study time is a possible alternative explanation, not a cause proven by the survey. Unresolved does not mean false, and self-selection does not establish app ineffectiveness. Two posts or an expert interpretation are not two studies.

Independent mini-case key

Administer A–D before the debrief. A–C are retained; D adds a different source structure and student-authored communication. The assessment examines a narrow application of evidence interpretation, not general misinformation resistance or open-web lateral reading.

Proposed reason rubric

  • 0: An attempted explanation uses irrelevant or inconsistent evidence or reasoning. Leave missing responses missing rather than scoring them as zero.
  • 1: Relevant evidence is mentioned, but its connection to the conclusion is absent or incomplete.
  • 2: Evidence is explicitly linked to a warranted conclusion and the relevant boundary of that claim. A concise sentence is sufficient; exact wording is not required.

Score judgments separately as 1 = matches key, 0 = does not; leave missing judgments missing. For Case A, naming 72/120 and linking it to “most respondents” is sufficient. For Case B, linking 12 < 16 to the reversed claim is sufficient. Neither descriptive case requires a causation disclaimer. For Case C, identify an alternative explanation or selection difference and connect it to the unresolved causal conclusion.

This is a proposed rubric, not a validated scale. Have a second scorer independently review a meaningful subset, discuss discrepancies, and document rubric changes. Report per-case results, missing data, and help received. Four cases are too few for a reliable general media-literacy or calibration score. Four responses from one student are not four independent participants. Report Case D’s three components separately from the A–C judgments and reasons.

Case D · Library hours: proposed component rubric

Source key: two data collections. Campus News and the Student Union newsletter cite Q17; Campus Radio cites the separately conducted R09. Scope key: the university-wide majority claim is unresolved because the volunteer polls are not established as representative. The results do not contradict that broader possibility.

Component0–2 scoring

Example acceptable update: “Most respondents in two separate volunteer polls favored later library hours. The three campus reports draw on those two polls, which may not represent the university’s student body.”

Leave skipped components missing. The online form permits explicit case-level skips; unsubmitted attempts and other omissions need the facilitator’s denominator record. Score attempted but incorrect reasoning as zero, not omissions. This is a proposed rubric, not a validated scale. Do not double-count D’s scope judgment in a combined score, or combine A–C and D into a general literacy scale.

A short debrief after assessment

  1. What was measured? Distinguish observed group averages from their explanation.
  2. Which word added a causal claim? Contrast “averaged” with “raises.” An accurate graph can accompany an unsupported interpretation.
  3. What did tracing add? Ask how reposts differ from independent data collections. The supplied trails are a simulation; real lateral reading checks beyond the original site.
  4. What did your note communicate? Compare a student draft with the model, without presenting the draft as automatically validated. Ask what a reader could reasonably conclude.

A separate fictional experiment

Students were randomly assigned to complete the same practice questions through an app or on paper, with equal practice time. Everyone completed the same later quiz. The app group averaged 14/20 and the paper group 11/20; the estimated difference was three points, with a 95% confidence interval from one to five points.

Ask before explaining: What makes a causal interpretation more defensible here than in FocusBoost? What would still be too broad a claim?

Instructor explanation: Random assignment provides stronger grounds for attributing the difference to the assigned practice condition. The finding still does not establish that every app helps every student, or that the benefit persists. The interval expresses uncertainty in the estimated average difference; it is not a range for every student’s benefit.

This experiment is a teaching contrast, not a revision to survey FB-01. Do not show it before the independent assessment. Future assessment forms should include varied observational and experimental designs so “causal claim” does not become a cue for “unresolved.”

Records, versions, and interpretation

Game CSV/JSON: one row per student attempt in CSV. It separates initial judgments/explanation (before supplied reasons), later reason/source selections, first/final headline choices, evidence-choice attempts, first/final evidence-note drafts and revision count, editorial action, and reflection mode/status/content. The first explanation and first saved note cannot be overwritten. No confidence scale, names, assigned study codes, timestamps, or click history are collected in the game.

Reflection: saved means two typed responses; external_reported means a paper/oral completion self-report with verification not collected; skipped means no reflection record was supplied. case_completed means the editorial action and reflection disposition are recorded, not that the student learned or the external response was verified.

Assessment CSV: four rows for one student’s A–D record. Case status distinguishes answered/skipped. Case D has fields for data count, shared-source explanation, scope judgment/reason, and student update. record_submitted differs from assessment_complete, which means all four cases were answered. Optional clarity/enjoyment/comment repeats across rows; analyze it once per student. JSON preserves the submitted record and export rows. Neither format contains automatic scores.

Facilitator record: track attempted/completed denominators, version, actual timing, major obstacles, assistance, external response verification, and any linking code used under your normal procedure. Game and assessment are separate records and are not automatically matched.

Both game and assessment now use new 3.2 browser storage keys. Earlier 3.1/3.0 records are not imported, overwritten, deleted, or relabelled. Export needed older records from the older files before replacing them. CSV formula-like cells are escaped; JSON retains original text. Browser storage may fail or be cleared; downloads and printing are not automatic submission.

Small pilot and claims

  1. Observe individual walkthroughs, including alternative response routes and relevant assistive technology. Record actual timing and assistance.
  2. In a class pilot, give new cases before discussion. Report judgments, reasons, source tracing, and communication separately.
  3. Have two scorers independently review a subset before reconciliation; document rubric changes.
  4. For delayed application, develop fresh cases for approximately one week later, varying topics, numbers, and source structures. Those additional forms are not included here.
  5. Separate feasibility, enjoyment, completion, immediate performance, retention, and transfer. Baseline measures and a suitable comparison are required for stronger improvement or causal claims.

The original SIB pilots did not evaluate this game. This package does not establish intervention effectiveness, a gamification benefit, lasting transfer, confidence change, or a PCT mechanism. It does not include a matched plain lesson, expanded alternate forms, consent collection, or automatic research uploads.

Technical and accessibility notes

Native controls, visible focus, dialog headings, readable chart alternatives, responsive layouts, and untimed operation are retained. Learning prose is larger in feedback. Paper reflection and a facilitator-supported oral route are available. Test your actual phones, browsers, screen readers, and printer. The facilitator guide contains open answer keys; this is not a secure exam.
