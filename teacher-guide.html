SIB:
The Evidence Lab

Seeing Isn’t Believing · First teaching prototype · EKU undergraduate psychology
One fictional investigation · Untimed · Version 1.0

Open the game

Students review a causal claim for a campus newsletter. At the Message Desk, they inspect three evidence cards, choose a conclusion and a reason, then review an explanation and a more accurate bulletin. The investigation-room illustration, notebook, hints, and local saves adapt the supplied CyberQuest format.

Keep the first pilot small.

Use this one case to find confusing wording, awkward controls, and problems with the answer choices or feedback. This prototype does not test general misinformation discernment, transfer, calibration, or lasting learning.

Prepare and facilitate

  1. Unzip the package and keep index.html and teacher-guide.html in the same folder. Open index.html in a modern browser with JavaScript enabled.
  2. Use one device per student for the first usability pilot. If students work in pairs, treat their submitted response as a pair response; do not count it as two independent observations.
  3. Check the actual classroom devices first, including local saves and printing. Local-file storage varies by browser. If files are blocked, use an institutionally approved static host.
  4. Introduce the mission without explaining the causal-inference answer: “Review the claim using the evidence cards, then choose the conclusion and reason you think are best supported.”
  5. Allow students to proceed at their own pace. Plan roughly 5–10 minutes for the case, plus a brief debrief; this is an untested planning estimate.
  6. If a student needs help, begin with the controls. The optional in-game hint asks: “What else could explain the difference between these groups?” Do not add an unrecorded answer explanation before their submission if you intend to describe first responses.
  7. Students finish by reviewing the feedback and choosing Add reviewed bulletin to notebook. A wrong first answer does not block completion.
  8. On a shared device, use Start over and confirm the reset between students. Print a notebook first if a copy is needed.

No research-methods prerequisite is assumed. Explain unfamiliar vocabulary neutrally if needed, and note that assistance in an approved evaluation. The interface has one active station; the remaining illustrated furniture is scenery.

Intended learning focus

Practice distinguishing an observed difference between existing groups from evidence that isolates a causal effect. Also practice distinguishing an unresolved causal claim from a contradicted one. Completion alone does not demonstrate either skill.

  • Supports: the available evidence establishes the claim for this task.
  • Contradicts: the available evidence establishes a conflict with the claim.
  • Unresolved: the available evidence does not settle the claim. This is a conclusion about the evidence, not a claim that nothing can ever be known.

Here, the claim is specifically “Using FocusBoost causes students to earn higher exam scores.” Do not silently switch the question to whether app users had higher scores, whether the app is useful, whether the study is authentic, or whether students should use the app.

The case and answer key

FocusBoost, the study, and every result are fictional. They do not describe EKU students or a real app evaluation.

“Using FocusBoost causes students to earn higher exam scores.”
Evidence cardWhat students seeWhat it contributes
A · ResultsApp users averaged 78 on an exam; nonusers averaged 70. Scores are out of 100.An observed eight-point group difference. No statistical-significance claim is made.
B · Study methodStudents chose whether to use the app. Researchers compared the existing groups without adjusting for other differences.The comparison does not isolate the app’s causal effect.
C · Other differencesApp users also reported spending more time studying.An alternative explanation is possible. This card does not establish that study time caused the exam difference.
Best conclusion: Leaves the claim unresolved.

Best reason: “The groups differed in app use and other ways, so this comparison does not isolate the app’s effect.”

The app users earned higher scores, but the groups also differed in other ways. The app might help; this comparison alone cannot establish whether it caused the difference. Do not teach “observational studies are useless” or “random assignment guarantees a sound conclusion.” The narrower lesson is that this particular comparison does not address other explanations well enough to settle the causal claim.

Possible responseFeedback emphasis
“Supports” or “the higher average alone establishes causation”The numerical difference describes the groups. It does not separate an app effect from other group differences.
“Contradicts” or “self-selection proves the app cannot help”A limitation in the evidence does not establish that the app has no effect.
Correct conclusion with an unsuitable reasonDiscuss the reason separately. A correct category alone does not show why the student chose it.

Reviewed bulletin

“FocusBoost users earned higher exam scores in this study; the study did not establish that the app caused the difference.”

Takeaway: When a claim says something causes an outcome, check how the study addressed other explanations.

Short debrief

  • What can we say confidently about the groups in this fictional study?
  • Why is “unresolved” more appropriate than “contradicted” for the causal claim?
  • What additional evidence would help resolve the claim? Discuss a well-conducted randomized comparison, relevant outcomes, follow-up, adherence, and other design limits at a level suited to the class.
  • What, if anything, was confusing about the activity?

Do not turn “unresolved” into a rule for all future claims. A later, expanded game should include well-supported and contradicted claims with defensible answer keys. This single case cannot evaluate that broader discrimination.

What is—and is not—being recorded

The game saves one local progress record: whether the case was started; draft answer choices; whether the hint was used; the first submitted conclusion and reason; whether the hint was used before that submission; and whether the bulletin review was completed. It does not record click histories, time-on-task, names, student IDs, confidence, or free text. No data are transmitted by the game. It has no research backend, analytics, login, or submission dashboard.

The notebook and printable report preserve the submitted answer before feedback. The game does not elicit a pre-investigation judgment or a revised post-feedback response. Opening a report before submitting does not reveal the answer. Reset removes the previous local case state after confirmation. An individual can reset and start again; the software cannot establish that a response is their first exposure.

Local storage is only a convenience. Clearing browser data, switching browsers, moving the file, private browsing, or blocked storage can remove or separate saves. On a shared browser, a subsequent user may see the prior notebook until it is reset. If saving fails, the game displays a notice and continues in memory. Print / save report uses the browser’s own print controls; nothing is sent to a facilitator automatically.

Useful question: Can students navigate the task, understand the causal claim and response options, and interpret the explanation as intended?

Descriptive evidence: Report counts of completed tasks, major usability barriers, and the conclusion and reason choices before feedback. Report the denominator and help provided. Keep individual and pair responses separate. An observed answer after receiving evidence is not a pretest; feedback exposure and completion are not posttests.

Limits: One guided case cannot establish an instructional effect, transfer, reliable individual differences, calibration, or lasting learning. A later learning evaluation would need multiple independent, defensible assessment cases and a comparison appropriate to the causal claim being made.

Research collection requires the applicable institutional human-subjects determination and approved procedures before it begins. If the instructor is also the researcher, address voluntariness and the relationship to grades through that process. The optional sheet below is a planning aid, not approval to collect data.

Relationship to SIB and PCT

This is a new SIB-inspired teaching adaptation. It retains attention to evidence and the limits of a judgment. The original SIB pilots do not establish this game’s effectiveness or a PCT-specific mechanism.

Version 1 does not include Personal Construct Theory elicitation, triadic comparisons, construct revision, confidence ratings, or calibration measures. It uses explicit evidence evaluation and explanatory feedback. Do not describe it as a validated PCT intervention. The earlier broader five-station proposal is a possible later expansion, not the scope of this package.

Accessibility and technical checks

  • Use the Message Desk hotspot or the equivalent Open investigation button. The illustration carries no evidence that is unavailable in text.
  • Tab and Shift+Tab move through controls. Enter or Space activates buttons and evidence summaries. Arrow keys navigate radio choices. Escape closes the dialog; draft selections are retained.
  • The layout adapts to narrow screens. Text can be zoomed. No required audio, animation, dragging, time pressure, or color-only answer cues are used.
  • Allow read-aloud support and extra time. For a paper alternative, present the claim and three evidence-card texts above, collect both choices, then supply the explanation.
  • Check keyboard, screen-reader, zoom, printing, and storage behavior with the specific devices and assistive technology used by students. Automated or single-browser checks do not establish universal accessibility.
  • Keep the first answer visible when discussing feedback. Do not score open-ended discussion automatically.

Files and adaptation

index.html contains the full game, embedded styles, JavaScript, and adapted room illustration. teacher-guide.html is this guide. START_HERE.txt contains opening and placement instructions. All game assets are bundled; no installation, API key, or internet connection is needed for the downloaded game.

The supplied CyberQuest code and illustration permitted classroom adaptation. This package reuses that illustration and visual direction with an original psychology case.
