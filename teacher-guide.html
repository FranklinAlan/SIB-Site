SIB Evidence Lab
Facilitator guide

Teaching goal: Distinguish a reported finding from an unsupported causal interpretation, and connect an editorial decision to its evidence.

Open the gameOpen independent mini-casesPractice response sheetResearch applied
Version 3 retains one fictional FocusBoost investigation. It adds a supported-claim contrast, a source-tracing action, explanatory feedback, and an evidence-linked bulletin. The independent mini-cases are separate from gameplay. This activity has not been validated as an effective intervention.

Prepare and run the activity

  1. Keep the supplied files together. Open index.html after extracting the ZIP, or upload the files to your existing GitHub Pages folder. The game works without a server or external assets.
  2. Start with a few individual walkthroughs. The review suggests 6–8 as a practical starting point for discovering usability problems, not a validated sample-size rule.
  3. Plan 15–20 minutes for the game and about 5–8 minutes for the optional mini-cases, plus a 3–5 minute debrief. These are planning estimates; record actual classroom timing.
  4. Give the neutral briefing below. Students make their first calls individually. If assessment is planned, delay peer discussion and the causal-inference debrief until their mini-case responses are saved.
  5. After the game, open assessment.html separately or provide its URL. Ask students to use only the new case information and record any help. For paper use, print that page before entering answers.
  6. Save any records required by your authorized classroom or research procedure. Then discuss the cases and invite questions. Use Start over and New attempt between students on shared devices; these resets are independent.

Neutral briefing

“You are reviewing a post for a campus psychology club. Some claims are supported by their evidence, some are contradicted, and some remain unresolved. Check what each claim says, inspect the available information, and decide what the club can responsibly communicate. Make your first responses on your own. There is no time bonus.”

Do not tell students to distrust everything or lower their confidence. Give interface help without revealing an answer; record reasoning help separately when using the assessment.

FocusBoost evidence key

All case details are fictional. App users averaged 78 and nonusers averaged 70 on the same exam. Students chose app use, app users reported more studying, and the analysis did not adjust for other differences. No sample size or uncertainty estimate is supplied.

PromptKey and reasoning
Users averaged eight points more.Supported. 78 − 70 = 8. The claim describes these observed means; it does not assert a statistically reliable population difference.
Using the app caused the difference.Unresolved. App use and other group differences were not isolated.
Reason“The score gap mixes app use with other differences between groups.”
Independent studies behind two postsOne. Study Sprint cites survey FB-01. Campus Catch-up cites Study Sprint.
Headline“FocusBoost users averaged eight points higher in this study.” The final bulletin adds the causal limitation.
Decisive editorial evidence“The group averages describe a difference; self-selection leaves its cause unresolved.” Both final actions are acceptable with this evidence.

Key distinctions: The graph is accurate within the fictional packet. The causal interpretation is not established. Reported study time is a possible alternative explanation, not a cause proven by the survey. Unresolved does not mean false, and self-selection does not establish app ineffectiveness. Two posts or an expert interpretation are not two studies.

Independent mini-case key

Give the assessment before the debrief if you want unassisted performance immediately after gameplay. The three cases use new names, numbers, and a plain reading format. They examine a narrow application of evidence interpretation; they do not measure general misinformation resistance.

CaseJudgment keyExample of a 2-point reasonCommon error
A · Reading-room pollSupported72 of 120 respondents is 60%, so most respondents selected movable chairs. The claim is limited to this poll.Rejecting the descriptive claim because the volunteers are not representative of all students.
B · Notes and recallContradictedThe handwritten group averaged 12 words and the typed group 16. The claim reverses the reported ordering.Treating a numerical contradiction as unresolved because the study does not establish causation.
C · Silence and musicUnresolvedStudents chose their condition and differed in sleep. The unadjusted comparison does not isolate silence as the cause.Calling the claim supported from the gap alone, or contradicted because sleep might matter.

Proposed reason rubric

  • 0: Missing, irrelevant, or inconsistent evidence or reasoning. Flag a missing response separately from an attempted but incorrect explanation.
  • 1: Relevant evidence is mentioned, but its connection to the conclusion is absent or incomplete.
  • 2: Evidence is explicitly linked to a warranted conclusion and the relevant boundary of that claim. A concise sentence is sufficient; exact wording is not required.

Score judgments separately as 1 = matches key, 0 = does not; leave missing judgments missing. For Case A, naming 72/120 and linking it to “most respondents” is sufficient. For Case B, linking 12 < 16 to the reversed claim is sufficient. Neither descriptive case requires a causation disclaimer. For Case C, identify an alternative explanation or selection difference and connect it to the unresolved causal conclusion.

This is a proposed rubric, not a validated scale. Have a second scorer independently review a meaningful subset, discuss discrepancies, and document rubric changes. Report per-case results, missing data, and help received. Three cases are too few for a reliable general discernment or calibration score. Three responses from one student are not three independent participants.

A short debrief after assessment

  1. What was measured? Ask a student to state the group averages. Distinguish a descriptive difference from its explanation.
  2. Which word added a causal claim? Contrast “averaged” with “raises.” An accurate number can be used in an unsupported interpretation.
  3. What did tracing add? Ask why three accounts were not independent corroboration. Explain that this was a supplied simulation; genuine lateral reading involves checking beyond it.
  4. What evidence would help? Discuss a well-designed randomized comparison, comparable study opportunities, adequate sample size, uncertainty, and replication. Randomization addresses selection differences; it does not automatically establish a universal benefit.
Contrasting example: “In a fictional sleep diary, the morning group averaged 7.5 hours of sleep and the evening group 6.5. The statement ‘the morning group reported one hour more sleep’ is supported as a description. ‘Morning classes caused an extra hour of sleep’ remains unresolved if students selected their class times.” Ask students to explain why both judgments can fit the same data.

Small, separate records

Gameplay CSV/JSON: Material version, first claim judgments, selected reason, source count, in-game hint use, first and final headline choices after feedback, first editorial evidence choice, evidence-choice submission count, final basis, action, and completion. Match-to-key fields describe this case only. The game stores no names, assigned study codes, timestamps, or click history.

Assessment CSV: Three rows per completed attempt with material version, optional instructor code, case ID, first judgment, short reason, reported help, completion, and optional game clarity/enjoyment/comment. Ratings repeat across rows for convenience; analyze them once per student. The JSON preserves the whole attempt. The student page supplies no answer key or automatic score.

Facilitator record: Keep completion/attempt denominators, material version, major usability obstacles, actual timing, and any help not captured by the student. Use the instructor-assigned code or another approved procedure if records must be linked; the site does not automatically match game and assessment files.

CSV cells starting with spreadsheet formula characters are escaped; JSON retains the original text. Local saving can fail or be cleared. Private browsing, device changes, and a new site origin can prevent resume. Downloads and printed sheets are not sent automatically.

What this pilot can support

A first classroom study can describe feasibility, usability, acceptability, and performance on these fresh examples. It cannot by itself establish improvement caused by the game, a benefit of gamification, lasting transfer, calibration, or confidence change. No confidence scale or PCT elicitation is added. The original SIB pilots did not evaluate this game.

For a later causal learning claim, develop and pilot a broader assessment and use a suitable comparison. To examine the game format, match the comparison activity on content, feedback, time, and assessment. A delayed assessment needs fresh cases and its own plan. Arrange any institutional review, participation, and record handling through your normal study process; this site does not obtain consent or upload research data.

Technical and accessibility notes

The files use native controls, keyboard operation, visible focus, dialog headings, readable chart labels, and responsive layouts. No timer, sound, dragging, login, third-party script, or internet call is required for gameplay. The printed response sheet offers a paper route. Test on the classroom browser and assistive technology you use. Answer keys are openly available in this guide, so this is not a secure exam.
